Competitive PUBG Mobile — squad playbook

Holding ground, winning the duel, reading the map

1What the 2026 map pool actually is

Before any map work: the tier-1 pool in 2026 is three maps, not five or six.

Both international events this year used the same rotation. PMGO Season 1 in June ran three matches on Erangel, two on Miramar, one on Rondo per day. PMWC 2026 in Paris in August ran a six-match day opening on Rondo, then a triple-header on Erangel, closing with two on Miramar.

Sanhok and Vikendi are not in that rotation. The structure around it changed too: Super League (PMSL) events were removed for 2026, and the ladder now runs through National Championships (PMNC) into four global events — PMGO S1, PMGO S2, PMWC and PMGC.

Practical consequence. If you are preparing for PMNC or an open qualifier, your scrim time should be split roughly 3 : 2 : 1 across Erangel, Miramar and Rondo, matching the tournament rotation. Time spent on Sanhok is time spent on a map you will not be scored on.
Check before you commit. Map pools are set per event and regional PMNC formats can differ from the global ones. Confirm against the official rules pack for your specific tournament rather than against this page.

2Anti-rush defence — eight categories

A rush succeeds because the attacker knows when the fight starts and the defender finds out. Everything below buys back that half-second, or makes the half-second not matter.

A. Crossfire hold default setup

Never put four players in one building. Split 2–2 across two structures whose entrances cover each other. A rushing squad can only breach one at a time; the second pair shoots them in the back while they are committed to the doorway.

Setup rule: the two positions must see each other's entry points, but must be far enough apart that one grenade cannot touch both. If a single frag can hit both pairs, you have built one position with a gap in it, not two positions.

Failure mode: both pairs holding the same approach. Crossfire means different angles on one lane, not two people watching one door from beside each other.

B. Layered defence early warning

One player sits wide as an outer marker, watching the two most likely approach lanes. Three hold the core. The outer player's job is not to win the fight — it is to call the rush direction three to five seconds early and fall back.

That three to five seconds is the entire difference between a defended compound and a lost one. It is enough time for the core to switch angles, pre-aim, and pre-place utility.

Failure mode: the outer player tries to trade. He dies, gives no callout, and you are now 3v4 and surprised.

C. Utility denial highest leverage

Throwables win compound fights more often than aim does. The principle: throw at the ground they must cross, not at the players.

  • Molotov the entry, not the enemy. A molly in a doorway or stairwell costs them five or more seconds and forces a second route — one you are already aimed at. Aiming a molotov at a moving player almost always misses; aiming at a door never does.
  • Smoke your own position, not theirs. Smoke is defensive. Use it to break an angle they already have, or to cover a reposition. Smoking them mostly blinds you.
  • Cook frags into cover. Throw at the rock or wall they are behind, not where you last saw them. Cook to two so it detonates on arrival.

Failure mode: saving utility for a fight that never comes. Grenades you carry to the final circle scored nothing.

D. Pre-aim discipline mechanical core

At close range the player already ADS on the doorway wins the overwhelming majority of duels. Reaction time is roughly a quarter-second; the raise-and-aim animation costs more than that again. Nobody out-aims that gap.

So: assign each player exactly one angle and forbid scanning. A player sweeping his camera around the room is a player who loses every duel he takes. Crosshair sits at head height for the distance being held, not at the floor.

Failure mode: everyone watching the "most likely" door. Cover the unlikely one — that is where a competent team comes from.

E. Sound denial and bait information war

When the rush is close, stop moving. Footsteps are the single largest information leak in the game, and a stationary defender is invisible to an attacker who is relying on audio.

Used positively: a deliberate noise — a door opened, a vehicle engine started and abandoned — pulls a rush into the lane you have prepared. Bait with sound, kill with the pre-aim.

Failure mode: repositioning "quietly" mid-rush by crouch-walking across floorboards. Either move before they are close, or do not move.

F. Vehicle-rush counter Miramar critical

On open maps the rush arrives in a car, because crossing open ground on foot is suicide. The counter is a split, not a focus:

  • Two players shoot the vehicle — driver first, then the engine block. A disabled car in the open is four players stranded with no cover.
  • Two players hold the dismount side pre-aimed. The dismount is a one-and-a-half second window where all four enemies are standing still and grouped.
  • Molotov the dismount point if you have the throw. It denies them the vehicle as cover and forces them into the open.

Failure mode: all four unloading into the car body. It absorbs the magazine, they dismount behind it with full HP, and you are all reloading at the same time.

G. Knock management where the kills are

This is the highest-value habit on the list and the most commonly broken.

Do not chase the knock. Hold the revive angle. A downed enemy is bait. The revive is where you get your second and third kill, because a reviving player is stationary, exposed, and has removed himself from the fight.

The mirror of the rule: when you knock someone, move immediately. They now know exactly where your shot came from and will focus that window.

Failure mode: pushing out to finish a knock, getting traded by his two teammates holding that exact angle for the same reason you should have been.

H. Refusing the fight IGL decision

Sometimes correct anti-rush play is to not be there. A won fight that leaves you at 40 HP with no utility, in a compound outside the next zone, with a third team inbound, is a loss recorded as a win.

Decision rule: if you cannot get all four players out of the fight, healed, and into the next zone within the shrink timer, do not take the fight. Rotate before they arrive.

This is where circle reading pays for itself. Knowing the next zone before you commit turns a coin-flip fight into a decision.

Five habits that lose defended compounds

  1. Healing during the rush. You are out of the fight for six to eight seconds and they know it.
  2. All four peeking the same window.
  3. Reviving in the open, in line of sight, under fire.
  4. Over-rotating to the first sound — that is often the distraction, not the rush.
  5. Reloading together. Stagger it; someone is always loaded.

3Weapons: close range, mid range, role loadouts

On tier lists. I could not verify the current patch's balance numbers against a reliable source. The specific damage and recoil figures circulating on top-up and affiliate sites contradict each other and several look fabricated. What follows is built on properties that do not flip patch to patch — calibre, fire rate class, recoil class, availability — plus a method at the end for measuring the current meta yourself instead of trusting anyone's list, including this one.

Close range (0–30 m)

For anti-rush the requirement is narrow: maximum damage in the first half-second, with minimal aim correction. Note that this is not the same as highest DPS on paper. In a pre-aimed hold, first-burst accuracy beats raw damage output, because you are already on target and the fight is decided in three or four rounds.

WeaponWhy / when
M416The default, and correctly so. 5.56 with a compensator, vertical or half grip and tactical stock puts the first burst exactly where you aimed. Available on every map. If you only master one gun, this is it.
Beryl M762The highest close-range damage output available from ground loot. Brutal recoil past three or four rounds, so it rewards short bursts at genuinely close distance. Give it to your entry fragger, not to everyone.
AKMHighest damage per bullet of the common ground ARs. Slower fire rate and heavy vertical kick. Strong in a doorway hold where you get one clean burst.
UMP45The balanced SMG. Controllable, forgiving, good inside buildings. A reasonable secondary for a support player in urban zones.
VectorExtreme close-range output, but needs the extended magazine to be usable and falls off hard past roughly 25 m. Specialist pick.
GrozaBest close-range AR in the game when it drops. Airdrop only, so it is a bonus, never a plan.
JS9Rondo-exclusive 9 mm SMG with low recoil and a full-auto mode. Genuinely useful inside Jadena City; irrelevant elsewhere.
ShotgunsSituational. One player holding one stairwell or one doorway, nothing wider. Not a squad answer to rushing.

Measure the meta yourself instead of trusting a list

You already have the habit from the zone work — do the same here. In Training Grounds, record your screen and measure time-to-kill against a target dummy at fixed distances for the three or four guns you actually consider. Ten trials each, take the median. Do it again after every balance patch. That is half an hour of work and it gives you numbers for your device, your sensitivity and your recoil control, which is the only version of the meta that affects your results.

4The 1v4 clutch

You cannot win a 1v4. You can win four 1v1s. Every rule below is a way of converting the first situation into the second.

The clutch loop

Assess → reposition to a one-way hold → go silent → wait for the commit → take the duel → reposition → punish the revive → repeat.

The rules, in priority order

  1. Read the situation before you move. Are they full HP? In zone or out? Did they just win a fight and burn utility? A 1v4 against four players caught outside the zone is barely a fight at all — it is a waiting game you are already winning.
  2. Get to a position with one approach. A single-entrance room, a rock with your back to a cliff, a stairwell. What you want is a place where their numbers cannot be applied simultaneously.
  3. Silence. This is the one people break. Stop moving entirely. They have to come and find you, and every step they take is information for you and none for them. Most 1v4 losses are a solo player repositioning at the wrong moment and getting heard.
  4. Let them commit. A squad hunting one player almost always pushes staggered — one impatient player first, or a 2+2. Wait for the first man past his cover, then take him.
  5. Reposition after every single engagement. The remaining three now know your window. Change it before they arrive.
  6. Punish revives, never chase knocks. Same rule as team play, worth even more here: a knock creates a stationary reviver and takes a second player out of the fight. Hold the angle on the body and you convert one knock into two.
  7. Use the zone as your fourth teammate. If you are in zone and they are not, do nothing at all. Hold and let the blue do the work.
  8. Smoke is for crossing and breaking, not for pushing. Use it to cross open ground or kill a sniper's angle. Throwing smoke to rush into is how solo players die.
  9. Heal only after you have broken line of sight and moved. Not between trades, not behind the same rock they last saw you at.
  10. Third parties are your ally. In a tournament lobby, a nearby fourth team is free pressure. Position so that they contact the enemy squad, not you.

When to invert and play aggressive

Holding is the default, not a law. Go aggressive when:

  • The zone is about to force you out anyway — then move first, on your terms, before it is urgent.
  • They have already located you and are setting up a coordinated push with utility. Holding a known position against prepared grenades is worse than flanking wide.
  • You are low enough that a passive hold just delays the loss. Take the wide flank and hit them while they are all looking at your last known position.

5Map analysis

Erangel 8 × 8 km — three of six matches

Character. The control map. Compounds sit roughly 200–400 m apart with usable cover between them, so fights happen at mid range and positioning beats raw aim. It is the most balanced map in the pool and the one where a disciplined team's advantage compounds most reliably.

Ground that matters. The Pochinki–School–Rozhok triangle is the centre of the map and the most valuable real estate in most matches. Georgopol gives you the container yard and apartments in the north. Mylta, Mylta Power and Novorepnoye anchor the east. Military Base on the south island holds excellent loot behind a bad commitment — the bridges are killing grounds and you need an early zone in the south to justify it at all.

Anti-rush here. Pochinki-style two-storey houses have two ground entrances plus a staircase, which is one entrance too many to hold alone. Never solo a house. Take two adjacent houses and cross their doorways. The real defensive terrain on Erangel is not the buildings at all — it is the stone walls, treelines and ridges between compounds, which give you cover with sightlines and no doors for anyone to breach.

Georgopol containers deserve their own note: sound is unreliable in the stacks, vertical angles dominate, and the correct hold is the top of a stack with a teammate watching the climb.

Rotations. Roads are fast and exposed. The treeline and ridge routes between Pochinki and School cost you fifteen seconds and save you from every sniper on the map.

Loadout. M416 plus Mini14 or SLR as the baseline. An AKM or M762 on the entry player for Pochinki-density fights. Bolt-actions are less valuable here than on the other two maps.

Miramar 8 × 8 km — two of six matches

Character. Long sightlines, sparse cover, and total dominance by high ground. The critical mental shift: on Miramar you fight for terrain, not for buildings. A hill with rocks on it is a better position than most compounds, because it gives you elevation, cover and sightlines without giving the enemy a door.

Ground that matters. Pecado sits centre with its boxing gym and casino. Hacienda del Patrón is the strongest single elevated compound on the map. Los Leones in the south-east is the largest urban area; San Martín, El Pozo, Chumacera, Impala and Campo Militar fill out the rest.

Anti-rush here. Rushes arrive by vehicle, because crossing open ground on foot against a set squad does not work. Run the split from category F without exception: two on the driver and engine, two pre-aimed on the dismount side, molotov the dismount point if you have it.

High ground rule. Take the hill that has a back exit. A hill with no retreat is a trap the moment the zone pulls away from you, and Miramar zones move further than they look.

Rotations. Vehicles are not optional. Keep at least one alive into the late circles and watch fuel — a dead vehicle in a late Miramar zone is a lost match. Route through valleys and behind ridges rather than along the open roads.

Loadout. This is the map where snipers genuinely earn their slot. M416 plus a Kar98k or M24, or plus an SLR if you prefer sustained pressure to burst. Smoke is worth more here than on any other map: it is the only cover that exists in the places you need to cross. Close-range weapons matter only inside Pecado, Los Leones and San Martín.

Rondo 8 × 8 km — one of six matches

Character. The largest playable landmass in the game and the most varied. The south-west is lakes and bamboo groves — dense, close-quarters, jungle-like. The north-east is ridges — open, elevated, Miramar-like. Jadena City in the south-east is vertical urban warfare. One map containing three different games.

Ground that matters. The major points are Jadena City, NEOX Factory, the Stadium and Test Track, Mey Ran, Yu Lin and Rin Jiang. Smaller but relevant: Tin Long Garden, Jao Tin, Hung Shan, Dan Ching, Nan Chuan, Hemay Town, Fong Tun, Long Ho, Mu Ho Pan, Bei Li and Rai An.

Two planes. Rondo runs two starting aircraft, which makes drop contests much less predictable than on the other maps. Scout both flight paths before committing — the compound you thought was uncontested may be directly under the second plane.

Destructible bamboo is the map's defining tactical feature. Bamboo looks like cover and is not — a frag or a vehicle clears it. Two consequences: never trust it defensively, and pre-clear it offensively to open a fire lane into a compound before you commit.

Anti-rush here. It depends entirely on which of the three terrain types your zone lands in, which is why Rondo punishes rigid teams. In Jadena, the escalators are both an ambush route and a loud one — hold the upper floor with a second player watching the escalator and the stairwell separately. In the south-west bamboo, treat it like a close-range map: tight crossfires, molotovs on approach lanes, and no reliance on vegetation for cover. On the north-east ridges, apply the Miramar playbook wholesale.

Loadout. The most flexible of the three maps by necessity. M416 plus a DMR as the base, then adapt: add a close-range option if the zone pulls toward Jadena or the bamboo, add a bolt-action if it pulls toward the ridges. The JS9 is a real option inside Jadena and nowhere else.

Maps outside the 2026 pool

Worth knowing, not worth scrimming, unless your specific regional format includes them.

Sanhok4 × 4 km. Very fast, dense jungle, constant contact. Rewards close-range weapons and aggression. Out of tier-1 rotation.
Vikendi6 × 6 km. Snow leaves footprints — a genuine information layer no other map has. Vehicles handle badly. Out of tier-1 rotation.
Livik, Karakin, NusaSmall maps built for fast queue times. Not part of serious competitive preparation.
